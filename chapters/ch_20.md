The Spire’s light was gone. The violet pulse had died, leaving only the sickly, flickering white of the streetlamps reflecting off the damp stone. The city felt smaller without that light pressing down on them. Colder.

Cassian walked with a limp he didn’t remember acquiring. His left knee ground against the femur, a dry, clicking sound that matched the ticking of the broken watch in his pocket. The street under his boots was a mess of shattered ceramic and splintered glass from the market stalls.

Kait walked ahead, her usual bounce reduced to a careful tread. She kept glancing at the shadows pooling in the doorways. Otho brought up the rear, her breathing a wet, rasping rattle. She leaned against the brick wall every twenty feet, pressing a hand to her ribs as if checking that they were still there.

"The Archives," Otho wheezed. She spat a glob of black phlegm onto the cobblestones. "You have a death wish, Cassian."

"We have a warrant," Cassian said. He didn't look back. He kept his eyes on the gap between the tenement blocks, where the iron girders of the High Sector blocked out the artificial sky.

"We have nothing," Otho said. "The Censors will be swarming. They’ll have sealed the Lower Districts by now."

"They won't," Cassian said. "They’re scared. When the Spire shakes, the rats run for the deep tunnels. Vex will be busy securing the power grid, not chasing three ghosts in the Slums."

He turned left, ducking under a rusted steam pipe that hissed condensation onto his collar. The smell here was different than the market. Less cinnamon, more sulfur. They were leaving the merchant ring and entering the Rust Belt. The transition was marked by the debris. Instead of rotten fruit and broken wood, the gutters here were clogged with stripped gears, broken pistons, and the occasional stripped bone of a Strata-Serpent.

"My feet hurt," Kait said. She stopped, kicking a pile of grey sludge. "And I’m hungry."

Cassian stopped. He checked his pocket watch. The hands were frozen at noon, but the weight of it grounded him. "You can eat when we’re inside. The Archives have stores. Emergency rations."

"Slurry?" Kait made a face. "I’d rather eat the rat."

"Slurry keeps you alive," Cassian said. "That’s the point."

He felt the needle behind his left eye again—a sharp, piercing heat. He rubbed the socket, blinking away the static. The Nausea was receding, but the echo of the deep time still vibrated in his marrow. He felt heavy. Displaced. Like he was a few seconds behind the rest of the world.

"Here," Cassian said. He pointed to a narrow fissure in the brickwork of a decommissioned laundry facility. "Service tunnel. It runs parallel to the main transit line. No sensors."

Otho squinted at the hole. "That’s barely wide enough for a child."

"It’s wide enough for someone who doesn’t want to be audited," Cassian said.

He squeezed through first. The brick scraped against his shoulders, snagging his coat. The air inside was stagnant, tasting of mold and old grease. It was pitch black. He felt his way along the wall, his fingers brushing against the slick slime of the tunnel.

"Hand," Kait whispered from behind.

Cassian reached back and found her small, calloused palm. He pulled her forward. Otho’s cough echoed in the confined space, sounding like a gunshot.

"Quiet," Cassian hissed.

They moved in a shuffle. Step, slide, breathe. Step, slide, breathe. The darkness pressed against Cassian’s eyes, playing tricks on him. He saw flashes of violet light that weren’t there. He saw the face of his brother, Joren, in the texture of the wall.

*Focus.*

He counted the paces. One hundred. Two hundred. The vibration of the city’s machinery grew louder, a low hum that rattled his teeth. They were getting close to the Central Column.

"Light ahead," Kait said.

It was a faint, dirty yellow glow coming from a grate in the floor. Cassian peered through the rusted iron bars. Below them was the Transit Ring. A massive cargo train, laden with excavated Chronite, rumbled past on magnetic rails. The cars were glowing with the sick green light of raw time.

"We’re above the Bore," Cassian said. "We’re close."

He found the maintenance hatch lever. It was rusted shut. He gripped the cold iron, braced his boots against the wall, and hauled. The metal screamed, high and thin, before snapping loose with a cloud of red dust.

He dropped down into the Archive antechamber.

It was a cavernous space, carved directly into the bedrock. The ceiling was lost in shadows, held up by pillars of black basalt. The air here was dry, preserved by the chemical scrubbers that hummed in the corners. It smelled of paper and dust.

"The Hall of Records," Otho whispered. She lowered herself onto a crate, groaning as her knees popped. "I haven’t been here since I was a girl. Before... before the sickness."

Cassian moved to the main console. It was a relic, brass and Bakelite, with rows of switches and vacuum tubes. He flipped the master switch. Sparks showered down. The overhead lights flickered to life, buzzing like angry hornets. Rows of filing cabinets stretched into the gloom, endless and regimented.

"Don't turn on the big lights," Kait said. She was already scouting the perimeter, checking the exits. "They'll see the smoke from the vents."

"It’s fine," Cassian said. "These lines are isolated. Independent grid."

He walked to the center of the room. There was a circular desk, the receptionist’s station, abandoned decades ago. A coffee mug sat on the corner, fossilized in a layer of gray dust. Cassian ran his finger through the dust. *Year 900. The Great Sanitation.*

"What are we looking for?" Otho asked. She was rummaging through a drawer, pulling out bundles of red twine. "The Queen’s journal? A map?"

"The Basalt Threshold," Cassian said. "Vex is drilling into it. He thinks it’s just a layer of hard rock blocking the Primary Vein. But the Queen... she put something there. A defense mechanism."

He started pulling drawers. *Geological Survey. 50-100. Structural Integrity. The Interregnum.*

"She locked the time," Cassian said. "She compressed the Chronite around the Spire to make it impenetrable. That’s why the city stopped sinking for fifty years. But Vex found a frequency that cracks it. We need to find the counter-frequency."

He found the file. It was heavy, bound in lead-lined covers. He heaved it onto the desk and opened it. The pages crumbled at the edges. Blueprints of the city. Cross-sections of the strata.

"It’s not here," Cassian muttered. He flipped faster, paper tearing under his haste. "It’s just geology. Rock density. Soil composition. There’s no magic."

"Maybe she didn't write it down," Kait said. She was sitting on the edge of the desk, swinging her legs. "Maybe she kept it in her head."

"She was a monarch," Otho said. She had found a bottle of water in a survival kit and was sipping it gingerly. "Monarchs do not rely on memory. They rely on bureaucracy. If she built a shield, it’s in the permits."

Cassian slammed the folder shut. He checked his watch. 12:00. Still. The hands were mocking him.

"Think," Cassian said. He pressed his palms into his eyes. The needle behind his eye twisted. "Vex is using sonic drills. Resonance. He’s vibrating the Basalt until it shatters. To stop him, we need a dampener. A silence."

"The Silentium," Otho said softly. She looked up, her eyes wide. "The cult. The ones who sew their mouths shut."

"They’re terrorists," Cassian said. "They blow up vents. They don’t have high-grade acoustic tech."

"They have the songs," Otho insisted. "My grandmother... she sang to us. Songs from the deep. She said they were the Queen’s lullabies. That if you sang them, the earth would listen."

Cassian stared at her. "You think a nursery rhyme is going to stop a Sanitizer drill?"

"I think the Queen understood frequency better than Vex does," Otho said sharply. "Music is just math, Cassian. You know that."

He did. He felt it in his bones every time he struck a rock. The ring of the hammer. The pitch of the fracture.

"Where are they?" Cassian asked. "The Silentium."

"The Under-Halls," Otho said. "Below the ventilation fans. Where the noise is worst. They go there to hear the Hum."

Cassian looked at the map on the wall. The Archives were centrally located. To get to the Under-Halls, they would have to cross the Transit Ring again.

"We need to move," Cassian said.

He grabbed the lead file. He didn't know why—instinct, perhaps. Or maybe just the desperate need to hold something heavy.

Suddenly, the lights in the Archives died. The buzz cut out instantly, replaced by the sound of boots on metal. Heavy boots. Clack. Clack. Clack.

"Hide," Kait hissed.

Cassian scrambled under the desk. Otho ducked behind a filing cabinet. Kait scrambled up the bookshelf, perching like a gargoyle in the shadows above the door.

The door to the antechamber groaned. Light flooded the room—harsh, white, electric light. The beam of a portable arc-lamp swept across the floor.

Cassian held his breath. He pressed the lead file against his chest.

Three figures entered. They wore white armor, polished to a sheen. Porcelain masks covered their faces. Sanitizers.

"Resonance spike detected here," one said. The voice was tinny, amplified by a vox-box. "Temporal displacement. High magnitude."

"Trash rats," another said. "Scavenging the ruins."

"Search the sector," the first one ordered. "Burn anything flammable. We can’t have the contagion spreading."

Cassian watched their boots through the gap in the desk. They were scuffed. Dirty. They had been walking in the Pit.

One of the Sanitizers stopped near the reception desk. The beam of light swept over the surface, inches from Cassian’s nose. He saw the dust motes dancing in the artificial glare. He saw a beetle scurrying away from the light.

The Sanitizer reached out. A gloved hand picked up the fossilized coffee mug.

"Year 900 ceramic," the voice said. "Contaminated."

The hand squeezed. There was a crunch. The mug shattered, pieces raining down on the floor. One shard bounced off Cassian’s boot.

"Clear," the Sanitizer said. "Move to the next sector."

The heavy boots retreated. The light swept away, leaving them in darkness again.

Cassian waited. He counted to one hundred. Then two hundred.

"Coast clear," Kait whispered from above.

Cassian slid out from under the desk. His legs were trembling. He stood up, bracing his hands on the wood.

"They're sweeping the area," Cassian said. "They know something broke loose."

"They're looking for us," Otho said. She sounded tired. "Or for the Queen."

"They won't find her," Cassian said. He looked at the lead file in his hands. "We need to get to the Under-Halls. Now."

They moved out the back way, through the blast doors that led to the ventilation shafts.

The air grew hotter as they descended. The Under-Hells were the bowels of the city, where the heat from the Mantle bled through the insulation. The walls were slick with condensation. The sound was deafening—a constant, rhythmic *thrum-thrum-thrum* of the massive air handlers pushing stale air up to the Spire.

"It’s loud," Kait shouted. She covered her ears with her hands. "How do they live down here?"

"They don't," Otho said. "They endure."

They walked for an hour. The path wound around the massive ductwork, suspended on catwalks that swayed over the drop. Below them, the darkness was absolute. If you fell here, you didn't hit bottom. You just fell into the fans.

Finally, the noise changed. It became less mechanical, more organic. A chant.

They reached a platform suspended in front of a massive intake valve. There were people there. Dozens of them. They wore rags tied with copper wire. Their mouths were sewn shut with coarse thread, the skin around the sutures raw and inflamed.

They were sitting in a circle, facing the center. In the middle of the circle was a woman. She was old, her skin like parchment, her hair long and white. She held a tuning fork in her hand.

She struck the fork against the iron railing. *CLING.*

The sound was impossibly pure. It cut through the mechanical drone of the fans.

The Silentium bowed their heads.

Otho stepped forward. Her knees buckled. Cassian caught her arm.

"Mother," Otho said.

The old woman looked up. Her eyes were sewn shut as well. She had no eyes, only smooth skin.

"Child," the woman rasped. Her voice was a dry rasp, escaping through the threads in her lips. "You bring the vibration of the deep time."

"We bring the need," Otho said. "The Censors are drilling the Basalt. They are waking the Thing Below."

The woman stood up. She moved with a jerky, marionette gait. She approached Otho, her face turning blindly toward her.

"The Silence is broken," the woman said. "The Queen’s shield is cracking. We can feel the tremor in our teeth."

"We need to stop it," Cassian said. He stepped forward, ignoring the glares of the Silentium. "We need the counter-frequency. The song."

The woman turned her sightless face toward him. She sniffed the air.

"You smell of the Pit," she said. "You smell of the dead."

"I am an Excavator," Cassian said. "I dug up the Queen."

The woman laughed. A dry, wheezing sound. "No. You dug up the memory. The Queen is not a person, boy. She is a frequency. She is the note that holds the city together."

She raised the tuning fork again.

"We cannot stop the drill," she said. "Metal is stronger than flesh. But we can change the rock. We can make the Basalt sing."

"How?" Cassian asked.

"The Harmonic," the woman said. "We must broadcast the Queen’s frequency into the bedrock. We must turn the stone into glass."

"We need amplification," Cassian said. "Big amps. The ventilation system."

"The fans," Otho said. "If we reverse the polarity, we can use the ducts as a giant speaker. The entire city will hear it."

"It will deafen everyone," Kait said. She was clinging to Cassian’s coat. "It’ll break glass."

"It will break the drill," Cassian said. He looked at the old woman. "Can you do it?"

"We have the note," she said. "We lack the power."

Cassian looked at the machinery around them. The massive generators driving the fans. They were old, inefficient, burning Chronite at a prodigal rate.

"I can reroute the power," Cassian said. "But I need time. And I need you to hold the note."

The woman nodded. "We have been holding the note for three thousand years. We can hold it a little longer."

Suddenly, the floor lurched. A violent shudder rippled through the catwalk. Dust rained down from the ceiling.

"They’re breaking through," Otho gasped.

Cassian looked at his watch. The hands were moving again. They were spinning wildly, counter-clockwise.

"Now," Cassian said. "Kait, help me with the breaker panel. Otho, get the Silentium ready. We have one shot."

Kait scrambled toward the junction box. She pulled a multitool from her pocket and started yanking wires.

"Red to blue?" she shouted.

"Red to ground!" Cassian yelled back. He vaulted over the railing to the main generator. It was a towering beast of black iron, pulsating with heat. "I need to overload the coupling. It’s going to get hot."

"It’s already boiling," Kait said. She stripped a wire with her teeth and twisted the copper ends together. Sparks showered down on her.

The Silentium began to hum. It started low, a vibration in their chests, but grew. It was a dissonant chord, ugly and grating, but underneath it was a melody. A sad, beautiful thing. Like a mother singing over a grave.

Cassian grabbed the primary lever. It was hot to the touch. He braced his shoulder against the iron.

"On my mark," Cassian shouted. "Three. Two. One."

He heaved the lever.

The generator screamed. A blinding arc of violet electricity exploded from the coils, lashing out like a whip. The sound hit them—a physical wave of pressure.

Kait was thrown backward. Otho fell to her knees.

The sound rushed into the ventilation intake, sucked into the maw of the city. It traveled up the shafts, into the walls, into the floors.

Cassian felt the floor dissolve. He wasn't standing on solid ground anymore. He was standing on a waveform.

He saw the city in his mind’s eye. He saw the layers of sediment. He saw the drill bit, miles above, grinding into the Queen’s layer. And then he saw the sound hit it.

The drill shattered.

Not broke. Shattered. It dissolved into dust as the frequency destabilized the atomic bonds of the metal.

The backlash hit the Archives like a physical blow. The glass in the windows blew out. The filing cabinets buckled, twisting into spirals.

Cassian hit the wall. Pain exploded in his shoulder. The world went white, then black, then a swirling, chaotic gray.

When he opened his eyes, the silence was back. But it was a different silence. A heavy, ringing silence.

He was lying on the grate. The generator was dead, a smoking ruin.

Kait was crawling toward him. Her nose was bleeding. Her eyes were wide, staring.

"Did we do it?" she whispered.

Cassian pushed himself up. His left arm hung limp. He looked at the old woman of the Silentium.

She was gone. Where she had stood, there was only a pile of gray ash. The others, the Silentium, they were all ash. They had burned themselves out to fuel the song.

Otho was sitting against the wall, holding her head. "My ears," she moaned. "I can't hear anything."

Cassian looked at his pocket watch. The glass had cracked, but the hands were stopped. Not at noon. At midnight.

"The Basalt is glass now," Cassian said. His voice sounded tinny, like he was underwater. "Vex can't drill through glass. It’s too hard. It’s too resonant."

"We won," Kait said.

Cassian shook his head. He looked at the pile of ash that had been the old woman. He looked at Otho, who was rubbing her ears frantically.

"No," Cassian said. "We just bought time. And we paid for it with blood."

He stood up, clutching his arm. The needle behind his eye was gone. In its place was a cold, hard certainty.

"We need to go," Cassian said. "The backlash will bring the Censors running. They’ll think it was an earthquake. We need to be gone before they realize it was a song."

He helped Otho up. She leaned heavily on him.

"Where to?" Kait asked. She looked small and fragile in the harsh light of the emergency floods.

"Up," Cassian said. He pointed toward the ceiling, toward the Spire, toward the impossible height. "We go to the Spire. We go to Vex. If the Queen is a frequency, then he is the only one who can hear her."

"And then what?" Otho asked.

"Then we ring the bell," Cassian said. "And we hope the city doesn't fall down."
