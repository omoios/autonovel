The floor wasn't just shaking; it was yawning. Cassian grabbed the edge of the console as the room tilted ten degrees. The map of Ouroboros on the floor slid, the ink lines rippling like spilled oil.

"Rising," Kait said.  "The whole city. We're going up."

"Not fast enough," Cassian said. He pushed off the console. The Nausea spiked, a hot wire dragged through the grey matter of his left eye. He blinked past it. "We don't have infinite time. The ascent generates drag. "

The Queen stood amidst the settling dust of Vex.  She looked at the hole in the ceiling. The sunlight was a physical weight on her skin.

"A helm," she said. The word sounded like a tomb door sealing. "You speak of flying a city that was meant to burrow."

"The gear-pits," Cassian said.  "Below us. The main drive shafts. If we reverse the polarity, we turn the drills into thrusters."

 

 "Some of us just haven't settled yet." He grabbed Kait by the shoulder. "You're the smallest. You take the vent shafts. Meet us at the primary junction."

Kait hesitated. She looked at the Queen, then at Cassian. "If I melt, I'm haunting you."

"Deal," Cassian said.

She scrambled up the debris pile, vanishing into a rusted grating in the wall.

Cassian moved to Otho. He slung the woman's arm over his shoulder. She felt light. Hollowed out by the Nausea, brittle as old parchment.

"Can you walk?" he asked.

"My knees are creaking," Otho muttered. "But they still bend."

  

"After you, little digger," she said.

Cassian half-carried Otho through the cloud of rust dust. The air pressure changed instantly. The smell of the Pit hit them—sulfur, burnt hair, and the copper tang of old blood.

They moved down the access tunnel. The gravity was shifting.  

"She's terrifying," Otho whispered, her breath hot against Cassian’s ear. "Does she... breathe?"

They reached the maintenance lift. The cage was hanging at a precarious angle, the cable frayed.

"No power," Otho said.

"We don't need power," Cassian said. He looked at the Queen. "We need mass."

 She grabbed the manual brake lever.

"Cassian," Otho said sharply. "The needle."

"If you fade down here..."

"I won't," he lied. He followed them into the cage.

The Queen pulled the lever. It wasn't a smooth descent. It was a violent drop, arrested by screeching brakes every twenty feet. The city cylinder rushed past them in a blur of dark iron and red pipe.

They fell for ten minutes. The air grew hotter. 

The cage slammed into the buffers at the bottom of the shaft. Cassian bit his tongue, tasting iron. 

A hiss of steam from the side tunnel. Kait dropped from a ventilation pipe, landing in a crouch. She was covered in grease.

"Flooded how?" Cassian asked.

"With water," Kait said. "Hot water. Boiling."

 If they were flooded...

"Can we drain it?" Otho asked.

"Too slow," Cassian said. He unclipped his utility belt, checking his tools. "We have to go through."

He led them into the main junction. It was a cavernous space, the ceiling lost in shadows of black pipe. The floor was a churning morass of gray water and black oil. The heat hit them like a physical blow, a wet blanket of steam.

In the center of the room, rising from the sludge, were the stabilizer gears. They were the size of houses, massive cogs of bronze and iron, currently spinning lazily. The sound was a low, bowel-loosening thrum.

"I can't swim in that," Otho said. She looked at the water with wide eyes. "The Nausea... the resonance in the liquid..."

"I'll go," Kait said. She stepped up to the edge.

"No," Cassian said. "You're too light. The current will take you. I need weight on the lever."

He looked at the Queen. She stood at the edge of the pool, staring into the murky depths.

"What is this fluid?" she asked.

"Recycled biomass," Cassian said. "People. Buildings. History. It's the slurry."

The Queen reached out. She dipped a hand into the black water. The water hissed. Steam rose, but not from heat. From time. 

"Foul," she said. She pulled her hand back. "You bathe the roots of the city in filth."

"It's fuel," Cassian said. "Your Majesty, we need to get to that lever. Can you... walk on it?"

The Queen looked at the sludge. She stepped onto the surface.

 She walked across the churning pool, leaving a trail of solidified time behind her.

"Come," she commanded.

Cassian didn't argue. He helped Otho onto the ice. It was slick, cold, and hummed with a low vibration. Kait scrambled ahead, fearless.

They reached the central column. The main clutch housing was a massive dome of brass. The manual override was a wheel ten feet in diameter, rusted shut.

"Seized," Cassian said. He gripped the spokes. He heaved. The muscles in his back screamed. The wheel didn't budge.

Kait joined him, pushing on the lower spokes. Otho placed her shoulder against the metal, gritting her teeth.

Nothing.

Cassian stopped. He breathed in the hot, oily air. The Nausea flared again, a white-hot spike behind his eye. He saw double for a second—the wheel, and a ghost of the wheel from a thousand years ago.

He looked at the Queen. "You have to break the loop."

The Queen approached the wheel. She placed both hands on the brass. She didn't push. She closed her eyes.

"The past wants to hold," she said, her voice echoing in the small space. "It does not wish to change."

She screamed.

It wasn't a sound a human could make. It was the sound of tearing metal, of mountains splitting. The brass casing of the machine groaned. Frost spread across the metal from her palms. The rust flaked away, turning to red dust.

The wheel began to turn.

"Push!" she cried.

Cassian threw his weight against it. The grinding was deafening, sparks showering down as the metal finally gave way. The wheel spun, faster and faster, until it was a blur of gold.

The room shuddered. The massive gears below them slowed. Then, with a clank that shook Cassian's teeth, they stopped.

Silence for a heartbeat.

Then, the gears began to spin in reverse.

"Stabilized," he said. He checked his chronometer. The hands had stopped spinning. They were pointing straight up. "We're level."

Otho slid down the wall, coughing. "We're flying," she said. "A city that digs... is now a city that climbs."

Cassian looked at the Queen. She was leaning against the railing, her skin pale, the gold veins in her neck dulling. Using her power had cost her.

"The Spire," she said, looking up through the grating in the ceiling. "We approach the roof."

"The Obsidian Eye," Cassian said. "We have to open it. Or we crash."

"Not crash," the Queen said. She pushed herself upright. "We break through."

She looked at Cassian. Her eyes were no longer obsidian. They were shifting, kaleidoscopes of years.

"You have done well, little digger," she said. "But the final ascent is the steepest."

Cassian felt a strange sensation in his chest. A loosening. He looked at his hands again. The fingertips were becoming translucent. He could see the pipes through his own skin.

"Cass," Otho said softly. She saw it too.

"You're fading," Kait said. She stepped closer, reaching out.

"Don't," Cassian said. He pulled his hand back. "If you touch me, you might slip too."

He looked at the Queen. "How long do I have?"

The Queen regarded him with a pity that was worse than anger. "Until the sun touches you," she said. "Or perhaps, until the sun touches the city. You are a creature of the deep. The light burns the shadow."

Cassian nodded. 

"Then we hurry," he said.

He pushed off the wall. His legs felt heavy, sluggish, as if they were turning to stone. He led them to the emergency ladder that ran up the side of the shaft.

They climbed.

"You're shaking," Otho said from below him.

"Just keep climbing," Cassian said.

They reached the Spire level. The air here was different. Thin. Cold. It smelled of ozone and perfumed smoke.

They emerged onto the promenade of the High Censors. The floor was polished marble, white and clean. The windows were vast sheets of glass, looking out over the vertical city.

And for the first time in history, the city was lit by natural light.

People were screaming.  The city's inversion had tossed their garbage back into their faces.

"Guards," Kait hissed.

A squad of Sanitizers stood between them and the Eye. They wore their porcelain masks and carried sonic rifles. They were leveling the weapons at the Queen.

"Step away from the... entity," the lead guard shouted. His voice wavered.

Cassian stepped forward. He placed himself between the guards and the Queen.

"I am Cassian Varr," he said. "Chronicler of the Deep." He held up his translucent hands. "I am the one who woke her."

"Fire," the captain ordered.

He kept walking.

The guards backed away, terrified. They lowered their weapons. 

"The door," Cassian said to the Queen.

She walked past him. 

She placed her hands on the glass.

"Open," she whispered.

The air rushed in.

It wasn't the recycled, stale air of the city. It was sharp, thin, freezing air. It smelled of dust and heat and something else—something green.

The sun.

It crested the horizon of the cavern roof. A beam of pure, unfiltered white light struck the Queen.

She gasped. Her body ignited. The gold in her veins caught fire. She became a silhouette of pure brilliance.

Cassian had to shield his eyes. The Nausea vanished, replaced by a blinding whiteness.

The city groaned one last time. The gears below them whined. And then, with a sound like a breaking chain, the city of Ouroboros cleared the cavern roof.

They rose into the open air.

Cassian fell to his knees. The light was eating him. He could feel his atoms unspooling. He looked at his arm. It was gone. Just smoke.

He looked up. The Queen was floating now, suspended in the beam of light. She was no longer stone. She was a woman of light, terrible and beautiful.

"Cassian," Otho cried. She grabbed his shoulders. Her hands sank through him. "Cass, hold on."

He tried to speak. He had no mouth.

He looked at Kait. The girl was crying, tears cutting tracks through the grime on her face.

"It's okay," he wanted to say. "We made it."

But he couldn't speak. He was becoming part of the air. Part of the light.

The Queen looked down. Her eyes were soft now. Human.

He looked at the city. It was a vast, jagged spear of iron, rising from the hole in the earth. It was ugly and magnificent.

He looked at Otho. She was holding his jacket, but he was no longer in it.

"Tell them," he whispered, though he knew she couldn't hear him. "Tell them it was real."

The city of Ouroboros flew on, leaving the dark behind, piloted by a ghost and a god.
